| **p. 422:** Five days later Raffles [Thomas Stamford Raffles] ordered Muntinghe and the High Court to resume full functions; Muntinghe requested dismissal rather than break his word, was briefly relieved, and then ordered by Raffles later that same day to resume full duties under compulsion (on 18 Sept. news of the Kali Tuntang capitulation had not yet reached Batavia). | **Page 422:** Details Raffles's three-step confrontation with Muntinghe five days later, ending with Raffles compelling Muntinghe to resume full judicial duties before news of the Kali Tuntang capitulation reached Batavia. |
| **p. 423:** Notes that Muntinghe later even applied for British naturalization (underlined in pencil). Contrasts Muntinghe with Dutch officials who swiftly entered British service—C.H.J. van Affelen van Saemsfoort, Capt. J.C.L. Meyer, Sec.-Gen. L.W. Meyer, Krawang *Landdrost* W. Offers, Buitenzorg palace intendant L.F.J. Villeneuve (appointed by Daendels [Marshal Herman Willem Daendels]; named his daughter Olivia Marianne Stamford Raffles Villeneuve on 10 May 1812), and Cirebon Resident M. Waterloo [Matthijs Waterloo]—alongside later friction involving J.D. Hartelieff (made *Drost* of Gresik) and Surabaya *Landdrost* P.A. Goldbach. | **Page 423:** Contrasts Muntinghe's initial scruples (though he later sought British naturalization) with Dutch officials who readily served the British, including Meyer, Offers, Villeneuve, and Cirebon Resident Waterloo, as well as Hartelieff and Goldbach. |
