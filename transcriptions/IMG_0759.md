## Annotated Transcription

| Original Text & Annotations | English Translation / Interpretation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **p. 426:** Profiles honorable exceptions who stayed aloof from the new regime: **J.C. Baud** (Janssens's [Lieutenant-General Jan Willem Janssens] secretary who fled with him to Buitenzorg and later worked under W. Robison), **D.W. Pinket van Haak**, **L.Z. Veeckens**, **J.I. van Sevenhoven**, and **J. Cock Blomhoff** (arrested and sent as POW).<br>*(Margin note: `Nahuys van Burgst`)* Highlights **Mr. H.G. Nahuys** (later Maj.-Gen. Baron Nahuys van Burgst), who volunteered for Janssens's staff, was wounded at Meester Cornelis, and steadfastly refused to break his oath even in captivity, being sent to Bengal as a POW (`"he bears a very high caracter among his countrymen for honor and uprightness"`). | **Page 426:** Contrasts self-serving defectors with principled Dutchmen—**J.C. Baud**, **Pinket van Haak**, **Veeckens**, **Van Sevenhoven**, **Cock Blomhoff**, and above all **H.G. Nahuys van Burgst**, who kept his word as a POW and was deported to Bengal. |
| **p. 427:** *(Top pencil note: `Nahuys - Honourable man`)* Concludes Verhoeven's essay (`Dr. F.R.J. VERHOEVEN`), contrasting the general mediocrity and self-interest of many Dutch officials under French-Batavian rule with rare exceptions of talent and character such as Muntinghe and Nahuys van Burgst. | **Page 427:** Concluding reflections by Dr. F.R.J. Verhoeven on Dutch civil servants' conduct during the 1811 transition, praising Nahuys van Burgst's steadfastness of character and chivalric fidelity to his given word. |
